The idea

The diagram is the model

Most process simulators ask you to build a second thing — a model that has to be kept in step with the diagram, and stops being in step the moment anyone is busy.

Diagramatix does not. The simulation numbers live on the BPMN you have already drawn: how long a task takes, who does it, how often a decision goes each way. Draw the process once, and it is ready to run.

What comes back is not an average. It is a distribution — the typical case, the near-worst case, and how much they vary — because the question a department actually asks is not “how long does this take?” but “what can we promise and keep?”

Setting it up

From a drawing to something that runs

The numbers on the process

  • How long each task takes — fixed, a range, or a real distribution: triangular, truncated normal, exponential, lognormal (the right-skewed shape service times actually have) or empirical, the observed values themselves
  • How often work arrives, and in what pattern
  • Which way each decision goes, as a percentage or as a rule about the case itself
  • Rework as a named percentage — what proportion comes back — with the cost of rework reported as its own line
  • A per-run activity charge for costs that do not scale with time, kept separate from staff cost because the two have different remedies
  • One panel showing every number at once, editable in place, with the gaps visible
  • Values the diagram already documents — effort, frequency — copied straight into the model, so a process that arrives drawn arrives runnable

The people who do the work

  • Teams with a real headcount, so work has to wait for someone to be free
  • Cost per hour, and an efficiency factor
  • Named people who hold skills, and tasks that require them — from an organisation-wide Skills list, or read straight off the ArchiMate diagram where a Business Architect already modelled who can do what. The middle ground between “nobody helps” and “everybody does everything”
  • Queue discipline per team: first-come, urgent first — with or without interrupting work already in progress — or shortest first
  • Working hours as reusable weekly shift calendars — a team that works nine to five processes nothing at midnight, and the simulation knows it
  • Holidays, shutdowns and absence on top of the weekly pattern: a Christmas close-down, a bank holiday, a summer with a third of the team away, against arrivals that do not stop
  • Standing team definitions kept once for the organisation, adopted by any project
  • Or harvested from the diagrams themselves — the lanes become the team library

Getting started quickly

  • Fill in what is missing — sensible starting values for anything not yet entered, across the whole drill-down tree rather than just the diagram on screen, and it never overwrites what you typed
  • Filled-in numbers look different from measured ones, so an estimate never passes for a fact
  • A readiness check before every run, separating what would mislead you from what is merely assumed
  • A gallery of worked examples to open and take apart — including Hire & Onboard, the capstone that uses all of it: calendars and holidays, named people with skills read from ArchiMate, priorities, a sweep, a tornado and a business case, in one process
What it models

Processes as they actually behave

The behaviour that makes real processes slow is exactly the behaviour most tools leave out.

Queueing

  • Work waits for a person, and the waiting is measured rather than assumed away — which is where most of the elapsed time in a real process goes
  • Queue lengths reported across the run, not only at the end
  • Service level reported per segment as well as pooled — a pooled p95 can look healthy while the urgent segment misses entirely

Several processes, one team

  • Run a whole portfolio at once with the teams shared between processes
  • So it can show one process starving another — what departments actually live with, and what a single-process tool cannot see at all

The full notation

  • Subprocesses, loops, and work that runs several times over in parallel
  • Timeouts and escalations that race the task they are attached to
  • Exception handling and compensation
  • Deadlines counted in working time — two working days is not forty-eight hours
  • A subprocess that links to another diagram is rolled in and run as part of the whole — the drill-down tree simulates as one process
  • Tasks marked as performed by a system rather than a person, so automation is a property of the model
  • Batches and cut-off times — “posted at 4pm”, “fifty at a time” — in the engine, set through a BPSim file today
What you get back

Four ways to see the answer

The heatmap

  • Your own diagram, each task tinted by how long work waits there
  • The brightest box is the bottleneck. There is no chart to interpret
  • Waiting inside a collapsed subprocess shows on its box, so nothing hides

The replay

  • Watch the work move through the process, piling up where the queue is
  • Stop it at any moment, add a person or push more work in, and let it run on — same past, different future
  • The fastest way to show someone why a process is slow

The report

  • How long a case takes: typical, near-worst, and the spread
  • How busy each team is, and a ranked list of bottlenecks
  • What the process costs in staff time
  • A plain-English summary written from the computed figures, and only those figures

The trace

  • Every case a row, every step a column, the time spent in each
  • Filter it, sort it, export it to a spreadsheet
  • For when someone wants to check the answer rather than take it
Asking questions

Scenarios, and changing your mind

A scenario is a version of the question. Change the team size, the arrival rate, the way a decision splits. A scenario stores only what differs from the original, so building the fifth one costs nothing.

You can also schedule things to happen during a run: put two more people on at nine, take a team out for four hours, double the arrivals for three days, drop a backlog in at the start. That is how you test a Monday morning rather than an average Tuesday.

Once there is a skills matrix, the question everyone asks becomes askable: what if we trained someone? A scenario gives a named person a skill, or adds a new starter, without rebuilding the team. And “what if we automated this task?” is one click — the task becomes system-performed and the run shows what that buys.

Every run is reproducible. The same starting point gives the same answer, which means a result can be checked, named, kept and compared against months later.

Pin one run as the baseline and every later run reports its trend against it, so a month of tinkering has a line through it rather than a pile of results. The warm-up — the stretch before the queues settle, which should not be counted — is chosen for you.

Defending the answer

A number you can take into a meeting

A simulation that says “17% faster” invites one question: is that real, or is it noise? The Simulator answers that itself, and the four questions that follow it.

Is the difference real?

  • Two scenarios compared with a proper statistical test over their replications, reported as a confidence interval
  • When it is not real, it says so — and offers to run the replications that would settle it

The curve, not the point

  • Sweep one number — team size 1 to 12 — and read where one more person stops buying much: the knee is marked
  • It refuses to invent a knee in a flat curve

Which assumption is load-bearing?

  • Every input pushed 20% either way and ranked by how far the answer moves — a tornado
  • Says where better data is worth buying, and where a guess is safe

Does the model resemble reality?

  • The simulated flow-time distribution laid over the one the business actually had, with a p50 / p90 / p95 table and an agreement figure
  • For a mined twin the check is out-of-sample: cases held back at import that the model never saw

The business case

  • Cost per case before and after, annual saving, one-off cost, and the month it pays back
  • A missing input is reported as missing, never treated as zero; exported to Word, Excel or PDF

What to try next

  • A ranked list from the study’s own runs — where every improvement so far came from, what never moved the answer, what has not been tried — each with its evidence, negative results included

Fitting in

You do not have to start from scratch

Build it from what really happens

  • Point Diagramatix Process Mining at a real event log and it discovers the process and its numbers
  • Real cycle times, real arrival rates, real routing percentages, and the hours the work actually happened in
  • A simulation of the process as it runs, not as it is documented

Take it with you

  • BPSim — the OMG interchange standard — in and out, so a modelling team can open your work in their own tool
  • Export a whole simulation as one file: diagrams, teams, calendars, scenarios
  • Send it to a colleague, or keep it as the record of what was decided and why
In short

A process you can argue with

A process diagram tells you what is supposed to happen. A simulation tells you what it costs, where it hurts, and whether the change you are about to make is worth making.

Diagramatix puts the second one behind a button on the first.
